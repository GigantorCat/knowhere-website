When analysing persuasive texts, constructive reading means identifying what rhetorical techniques the writer employs and understanding how each functions within the argument's architecture. You're mapping the blueprint—recognising where ethos establishes authority, where logos builds logical scaffolding, and where pathos creates emotional resonance. This reading mode asks "how does this text work?" rather than "do I agree?" Critical reading then evaluates the effectiveness and ethics of these choices: does the emotional appeal substitute for weak evidence? Does the credibility claim rest on genuine expertise or borrowed authority? Are logical connections sound or merely plausible-sounding? Both modes are essential—you must first understand the mechanism before you can assess its integrity.
Know This
Effective argument analysis requires recognising that persuasion operates through strategic linguistic construction—every word choice, every appeal, every structural decision positions you toward a particular conclusion before logic even enters the room. Beyond the three appeals, name the quiet machinery: inclusive pronouns that enrol the reader without making a checkable claim, figurative devices that argue underneath the evidence, and tone as a two-way stance — toward the subject and toward the audience — running anywhere from objective and formal to openly passionate.

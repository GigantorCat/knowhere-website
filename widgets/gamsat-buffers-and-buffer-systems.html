A buffer doesn't stop the attack — it absorbs it

Most students think buffers block pH change like a wall. In reality, a buffer is a sacrificial pair: a weak acid and its conjugate base that soak up incoming H⁺ or OH⁻ by reacting with them, holding pH steady until one half runs out. Add strong acid or base and watch the buffer respond.

When you add strong acid (H⁺), the conjugate base A⁻ grabs it to form HA, removing the threat. When you add strong base (OH⁻), the weak acid HA donates H⁺ to neutralise it, forming A⁻ and water. Each half of the pair sacrifices itself to absorb the opposite attack. The buffer holds pH steady as long as neither component runs out. Once one is used up, the buffer capacity is exceeded and the pH swings sharply: down if acid keeps coming, up if base does. This is Le Chatelier in action: the equilibrium HA ⇌ H⁺ + A⁻ shifts left or right to consume the added species, but the shift is small enough that [H⁺] barely moves.

Know This

A buffer is one solution containing both a weak acid and its conjugate base, allowing the pair to absorb added H⁺ or OH⁻ until one component is consumed.

Social media isn't eroding democracy — it's exposing cracks that were always there. For decades, we trusted gatekeepers to filter public discourse, but that trust was built on limited access, not genuine consensus. A 2021 Reuters study found that only 29% of Australians trust traditional news media, down from 44% in 2018. When platforms democratised publishing, they didn't create division — they revealed it. Critics argue that algorithms amplify extreme voices, and they're right — but suppressing those voices doesn't eliminate the beliefs, it just drives them underground. The solution isn't less access but better literacy: teaching audiences to interrogate sources, recognise bias, and demand accountability from all publishers, traditional or otherwise. If democracy feels fragile now, perhaps it's because we're finally seeing it clearly.

When constructing an argument, your contention is the lens through which everything else is filtered — not just a claim, but a reframing of the issue that makes your position feel inevitable. Sequence your supporting arguments to build momentum: start with the most accessible point to establish credibility, escalate to more complex claims once you've earned trust, and position your strongest evidence where it reinforces emerging patterns rather than standing alone. Choose evidence that resonates with your specific audience's values and priorities — the same statistic lands differently depending on whether you're addressing educators, policymakers, or peers. Strong arguments don't avoid counter-positions; they anticipate objections and address them directly, using concessions strategically to show you've considered complexity while ultimately reinforcing your central claim.
Know This
Persuasive writing succeeds when readers feel they've discovered your contention themselves — sequence builds that path, evidence makes it credible, and addressing counter-arguments proves you're not hiding anything.

Fruit smells are esters, and making them in a flask hits an equilibrium trap

Esters give bananas, pineapples, and strawberries their signature scents, but the lab reaction that makes them sits stubbornly in the middle, never fully finishing. Esterification is a reversible condensation: acid meets alcohol, a sulfuric acid catalyst speeds it up, heat gets it to equilibrium sooner, and a reflux apparatus stops the reactants and product evaporating before equilibrium is reached. Turn up the heat and watch the mixture reach the same roughly two-thirds yield faster, then choose Excess Alcohol to start a batch with three times as much alcohol and watch the yield settle higher.

Reflux is the esterification lifeline: the condenser above the flask captures evaporating alcohol and ester vapour, cooling them back to liquid before they escape. Without it, you would lose reactants and products to the air before equilibrium could even establish. The technique lets you heat the mixture vigorously for hours without losing mass, giving the slow reversible reaction time to creep toward a useful yield. Heat does not shift the equilibrium position, though: esterification is almost thermoneutral, so heating speeds up the forward and reverse reactions by the same factor and simply gets you to the same roughly 67% yield sooner, and because the reverse reaction keeps operating you never get 100% conversion.
Know This
Esterification is a reversible condensation that reaches equilibrium at roughly two-thirds conversion, so reflux heating gets it there faster without losing material, and excess reactant is what pushes the yield higher.

How revolutionary was France: weigh what changed against what persisted in four areas, 1789 to 1795

Liberty declared, inequality remained

The Revolution abolished feudalism and proclaimed equality—yet by 1795 the poor were still poor, women had no vote, and stability was nowhere. Some changes were genuine. Others were promises on parchment. Step the slider through four areas of French life and weigh what changed against what persisted.

Feudalism and legal privilege were genuinely abolished—the seigneurial system, tithes, and noble exemptions vanished permanently. Equality before the law became enshrined in new legal codes. The metric system, civil marriage, and centralised administration replaced centuries of regional chaos. These were structural transformations, not rhetoric. By 1795 France had a republic, a professional bureaucracy, and a society where birth no longer guaranteed power. The revolution demolished the ancien régime's legal architecture and never rebuilt it.

Know this

The Revolution was structurally radical but socially incomplete—it destroyed feudalism and legal privilege while leaving women’s exclusion from politics and wide economic inequality largely in place.

In this picture, 2A ⇌ P turns two gas particles into one, so squeezing the box (Pressure ×2) shifts it toward P. When you increase pressure on a gaseous equilibrium, the system shifts toward the side with fewer gas moles — because that partially relieves the pressure you just applied. For example, in the Haber process (N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃), the left has 4 gas moles and the right has 2. Increasing pressure shifts right, making more ammonia. But the pressure never drops back to its original value — the shift only partially opposes your change. This is why industrial ammonia synthesis runs at 200 atmospheres: the equilibrium favours products under high pressure, even though the system fights back by making some.
Know This
Only temperature changes K itself — pressure and concentration changes shift the position but leave the equilibrium constant unchanged.

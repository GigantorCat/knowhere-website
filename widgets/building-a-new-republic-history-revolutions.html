The stronger the government, the deeper the compromise

But the Constitution that replaced it in 1787 wasn't a triumph of ideals — it was a patchwork of compromises, and the price of a stronger federal union was the entrenchment of slavery. The Articles of Confederation established a "league of friendship" where states retained sovereignty and the central government had no power to tax, regulate commerce, or enforce laws. Congress couldn't even compel states to contribute funds. This weakness made it impossible to pay war debts, quell uprisings like Shays' Rebellion, or conduct foreign policy. The system was designed to prevent tyranny, but it created paralysis instead. By 1787, the consensus was clear: a functioning nation needed a stronger centre.
Know This

The Constitution of 1787 created a stronger federal system with the power to tax and regulate commerce, but the price was constitutional protection for slavery — the Three-Fifths Clause, the fugitive slave provision, and the 20-year protection of the slave trade were the compromises required to get Southern states to ratify.

Taxes and tradeable permits let firms self-select: those who can reduce pollution cheaply do so, while high-cost firms pay the tax or buy permits. The result is cost-effective abatement—society reaches the target at minimum total cost. Subsidies for clean technology can work similarly, though they risk encouraging entry by less-efficient firms just to claim the subsidy. The innovation incentive is key: a firm paying a tax saves money by inventing cleaner processes, whereas a firm meeting a fixed standard has no economic reason to exceed it. Carbon leakage remains the Achilles heel—if permits only apply domestically, energy-intensive production moves offshore and global emissions barely budge.
Know This
The optimal tax equals the marginal external cost at the efficient quantity, which is never zero pollution—because the last unit of abatement costs more than the damage it prevents, so society is better off tolerating that final increment.
